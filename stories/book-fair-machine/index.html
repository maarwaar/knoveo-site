TheBook FairMachine

How the Scholastic Book Fair makes money: what the school gets, cash or Scholastic Dollars, and how Scholastic earns from it.

The short answerScholastic's book fairs brought in $576.0 million in its 2026 financial year, about 36 cents of every dollar of its $1,581.9 million revenue. The school provides the room, the staff and the customers, and Scholastic provides the books, the cases and the trucks. On a fair with $4,000 or more in sales, the school chooses 25% in cash or 50% in Scholastic Dollars, which are spent in Scholastic's own catalog or at the school's next fair. Scholastic says fairs raise more than $200 million a year for schools, and many schools choose Scholastic Dollars on purpose. In the same year the whole company made an operating profit of about $15 million, and the children's book division, which holds the fairs, made $143 million.

Questions

Do schools make money from the Scholastic Book Fair?

Yes. Scholastic says fairs raise more than $200 million a year for schools. On a fair with $4,000 or more in sales, a school gets 25% in cash or 50% in Scholastic Dollars. For an average fair of about $6,000, that is about $1,500 in cash or about 3,000 Scholastic Dollars. Scholastic Dollars are spent in Scholastic's own catalog or at the school's next fair. Smaller fairs earn a smaller share.

How does Scholastic make money from book fairs?

The school provides the room, the staff and the customers, and Scholastic provides the books, the cases and the trucks. Fairs brought in $576.0 million in its 2026 financial year, up from $548.3 million the year before. A field team of about 570 people books and supports them, and Scholastic has also grown by buying rivals, including the biggest one in 1998 for about $10 million.

What are the criticisms of the Scholastic Book Fair?

Librarians told Education Week in 2024 that students split into those who could buy and those who could not, and one researcher said parents on low incomes called it their most embarrassing experience. Some librarians have complained of fewer titles and more merchandise. Scholastic says those items have been cut back, that a school can ask for a books-only fair, and that its giving programs give away more than a million books a year.

The whole story

The narration of the film, word for word. Numbers link to their sources.

Cold open

This is an empty school gym.

On Monday morning, a truck pulls up outside.

Out roll the silver cases.

They swing open. And the gym becomes a bookstore.

If you went to school in America, you probably remember this week.

In about a week, an average fair sells around six thousand dollars' worth. 1

The store pays no rent.

The people at the till are librarians and parent volunteers. 2

And the school's reward often comes in a currency that only one company accepts.

So who really makes money at the school book fair?

This is the business inside the gym.

This is the book fair machine.

The Numbers

Start with the company behind it.

Scholastic is a publisher. In the United States, it publishes Harry Potter, Dog Man and The Hunger Games. 3

You would guess the money comes from bestsellers.

In its 2026 financial year, Scholastic's revenue was about one point six billion dollars. 4

Five hundred seventy-six million of that came from book fairs. 5

That is thirty-six cents of every dollar. 6

From pop-up shops in school gyms.

And look at the profit. The whole company made an operating profit of about fifteen million dollars. 7

The children's book division, which holds the fairs, made one hundred forty-three million. 8

That division also includes book clubs and bookstore sales. But the fairs are its biggest piece. 9

Other parts of the company lost money. 10

And the fairs are still growing. Revenue rose five percent in a year, with more fairs, and more money per fair. 11

It is also a seasonal machine. In summer, when schools close, it nearly stops. 3

So the gym is not a side business. The gym is the engine.

How It Works

So how does a fair actually work?

The school picks a week. Scholastic sends a truck. 12

The cases arrive already packed with books, sorted by age. 12

Volunteers wheel them in, and set them up in a U shape. 12

A school gets a few hundred different titles. 2

The cash registers usually belong to Scholastic. 2

The staff do not.

If a shelf runs low during the week, the school can ask for a restock. 12

About seventy percent of fairs are run by the school librarian. 2

The rest are run mostly by parent volunteers. 2

Children come class by class. Flyers go home. Parents send money.

Then the cases close, the truck comes back, and the fair moves to the next school.

Scholastic says it runs around a hundred thousand fairs a year. 13

And reaches more than thirty million children. 14

Behind them is a field team of about five hundred seventy people, booking and supporting fairs. 3

Think about what that means for costs.

A normal bookstore pays rent, wages, and marketing.

Here, the school provides the room, the staff, and every customer.

What Scholastic provides is the books, the cases, and the trucks. 3

The Deal

So what does the school get?

Take an average fair. About six thousand dollars in sales. 2

Some small schools take in about twelve hundred. A few very large ones reach a hundred thousand. 2

The school has two choices.

Choice one. Cash. For a fair this size, twenty-five percent. 15

That is about fifteen hundred dollars for the school. 2

Choice two. Fifty percent. Double. 16

But not in dollars. In Scholastic Dollars. 17

Three thousand of them. 2

They are spent in Scholastic's own catalog, or at the school's next fair. 17

So watch what happens. Scholastic keeps all the cash.

And the school's share comes back to Scholastic, to be swapped for more of its books. 17

A dollar of books costs Scholastic less than a dollar of cash. 18

To be fair, schools are not being tricked. Many choose this on purpose.

A library with no book budget gets twice as many books this way.

Scholastic Dollars earned from a fair's sales do not expire. 17

Bonus rewards are different. Those expire six months after the fair starts. 17

And there are extra rewards for schools that hold more than one fair a year. 17

Every one of those rules points the same way. Come back, and spend it here.

But smaller fairs earn a smaller share. 19

Across the country, Scholastic says fairs raise more than two hundred million dollars a year for schools. 20

Which tells you how much money the fairs take in.

The Moat

So why does nobody else do this?

Scholastic did not invent the book fair. It bought its way in.

In 1981, it bought a company called California School Book Fairs. 21

Then it kept buying. In 1998, it took over the fairs of its biggest rival, for about ten million dollars. 22

That rival had run nineteen thousand fairs the year before. 23

In 2019, it bought another one, a family company in California. 24

What it built is very hard to copy.

Warehouses across the country. Its own trucks. And close to two thousand warehouse workers. 25

And a relationship with tens of thousands of schools, renewed every year.

In 2017, a big school supplier called Follett launched its own book fairs. 26

It started with one hundred eighty fairs, and had thousands booked by 2020. 27

Then the pandemic closed the schools. 28

Four years later, it stopped. 29

A startup called Literati took over that business. 28

In 2026, Literati said its fairs bring in nearly forty million dollars a year. 30

Scholastic's fairs bring in more than fourteen times that. 31

The moat is not the books. Anyone can buy books.

The moat is the truck, the case, and the school's calendar.

The Kids

Now look at it from the child's side.

A fair is exciting. For many children, it is the only bookstore they ever walk into.

But the customers are six to twelve years old, shopping with their classmates watching.

Librarians told Education Week they saw students split into two groups. Those who could buy, and those who could not. 32

One researcher said parents on low incomes called it their most embarrassing experience. 33

Scholastic's answer is a giving program, called Share the Fair, and more books at five dollars or less. 34

It also runs sponsored fairs, where every child takes home books for free. Scholastic says these programs give away more than a million books a year. 14

A million is a lot of books. It is also a small slice of the total.

And some librarians have gone further, and built free book fairs of their own. 32

Then there is the stuff that is not books.

Novelty pens. Erasers. Posters.

Some librarians have complained of fewer titles and more merchandise. 35

Scholastic says those items have been cut back, and that a school can ask for a books-only fair. 35

And cash is fading. Parents can now load money onto an e-wallet before the fair. 36

If a child does not spend it all, the rest becomes a Scholastic gift card balance. 37

If you watched our film on gift cards, you know what an unspent balance is worth to a company.

It can be used at the next fair, or donated to the school's giving fund. 38

One more thing. A fair is not a neutral shelf. Somebody chooses what goes in the cases.

In 2023, Scholastic put sixty-four titles, many dealing with race and gender, into a separate case that schools could opt out of. 39

Scholastic said it was responding to new state laws on school books. Authors and librarians protested. 39

Within days, Scholastic apologized, called it a mistake, and ended the separate case. 40

Whoever fills the cases decides what thirty million children see.

For You

So what does all this mean for you?

One. Agree a budget with your child before the fair, not at the till.

Two. Ask your school which reward it takes. Cash, or Scholastic Dollars.

Three. Check for leftover e-wallet money. Use it, or give it to the school's fund. 36

Four. If the toys bother you, ask the school about a books-only fair. 35

And if you are a teacher or a librarian, do the math on cash against credit, for what your school actually needs.

And five. If you can, give a little to the fund for the children who cannot buy.

The Idea

The book fair looks like a school event.

It is really a store. With free rent, free staff, and customers delivered class by class.

It pays its landlord partly in its own money.

And it moves on before the week is over.

It is also how millions of children get to pick out a book of their own.

Both things are true.
