How gift cards make money: who gets what from a $100 card, and the company behind the supermarket rack.

Questions

Why do supermarkets sell gift cards for other stores?

Because the rack pays. In 2017 Blackhawk collected about $9.20 per $100 loaded on its US racks, and about 60% of that went to the host stores, roughly $5.50 on a $100 card. These are averages, and every deal differs. The rack also takes almost no space, nothing on it can spoil, and a card is only switched on when the cashier scans it and you pay.

What happens to unused gift cards?

Companies plan for it. They study how old cards get used, estimate the share that never will be, and count that share as sales. The industry calls it breakage. Starbucks counted more than $220 million this way in its 2025 financial year. In some states, long-forgotten balances must be handed to the state as unclaimed property.

Do gift cards expire?

Under a 2009 federal law, the money on a gift card cannot expire for at least five years, and inactivity fees are banned for the first 12 months. Some states go further. In California, most store gift cards can never expire.

The whole story

The narration of the film, word for word. Numbers link to their sources.

Cold open

Watch this gift card.

Fifty dollars. Scanned at a supermarket checkout.

In that one beep, three companies just got paid.

And nobody has bought anything yet.

The store that sold it does not even own the brand on the card.

And if this card ends up forgotten in a drawer, somebody else keeps the money.

In 2024, a survey estimated that Americans were holding about twenty-seven billion dollars in unused gift cards. 1

So who really makes money on a gift card?

And why does your supermarket sell gift cards for other stores?

This is the business behind the rack.

This is the gift card machine.

The Rack

Start with the rack itself.

Coffee.

Video games.

Dinner.

Home improvement.

A steak dinner, hanging next to the chewing gum.

Dozens of brands, hanging in a store that sells none of them.

This rack was not invented by a bank, or by a tech company.

It came out of a supermarket.

In two thousand one, the grocery chain Safeway set up a small division, to sell other companies' gift cards in its own stores. 2

That division was called Blackhawk Network. 3

The idea worked. Soon, other grocery chains wanted the same rack.

So Blackhawk became the middleman. It signs up the brands, and it signs up the stores.

By 2017, its cards hung in more than forty-six thousand stores across the United States. 3

And it carried more than nine hundred brands. 3

Safeway spun the company off in 2014. Most shoppers have still never heard its name. 3

Outside the United States, it reached more than two hundred thousand more locations, in twenty-five other countries. 3

The Split

So follow the money on a single card.

Say you load one hundred dollars onto a restaurant gift card, at the supermarket.

The restaurant does not get one hundred dollars.

In 2017, Blackhawk collected about nine dollars and twenty cents for every hundred loaded on its American racks. 4

That money comes from the brands on the cards, as commission, and from fees on prepaid cards. 3

Then Blackhawk shares it. About sixty percent went straight to the stores that host the racks. 5

So on a hundred dollar card, the supermarket earned about five dollars and fifty cents. 6

Blackhawk kept less than four.

And the restaurant ended up with about ninety-one dollars.

These are averages. Every deal is negotiated. 3

The brand also gets something money alone cannot buy. A place in front of every shopper, every week.

Now you can see why the supermarket loves this rack.

It takes almost no space. Nothing on it can spoil.

And a card is only switched on when the cashier scans it, and you pay. 7

About five dollars, for hanging up a piece of plastic.

But why would a brand pay to hang in someone else's store?

Money Now

The first reason is timing.

When you buy a gift card, the brand gets its money up front.

It hands over the coffee, or the dinner, weeks or months later.

Until then, your money sits with the company.

Look at Starbucks, which runs its own cards and its own app.

At the end of its 2025 financial year, Starbucks reported about one point eight billion dollars in card balances and other payments received in advance. 8

That is money from customers, for coffee they have not ordered yet.

In effect, an interest-free loan.

No bank. No paperwork. No interest.

And it peaks every winter. Starbucks says its cards are mostly loaded during the holiday season. 9

That lifts its cash flow at the start of every financial year. 9

The Drawer

The second reason is the drawer.

Some cards are never used. The industry has a word for that money. Breakage.

In 2024, a Bankrate survey found that forty-three percent of American adults had at least one unused gift card. 1

On average, each of them was sitting on two hundred forty-four dollars. 1

Added up, about twenty-seven billion dollars. 1

And the pile keeps growing. In 2021, the average was one hundred sixteen dollars. 1

The households that leave the most behind are the wealthiest ones. 1

Companies plan for this. They study how old cards get used, and estimate the share that never will be. 10

Then they count that share as sales.

In its 2025 financial year, Starbucks counted more than two hundred twenty million dollars this way. 11

Revenue, for coffee nobody drank.

The Law

It used to be worse.

Many gift cards used to expire, or lose value to inactivity fees.

So in two thousand nine, Congress stepped in. 12

Today, the money on a gift card cannot expire for at least five years. 12

And inactivity fees are banned for the first twelve months. 12

Some states go further. In California, most store gift cards can never expire. 13

And in some states, long-forgotten balances must be handed to the state, as unclaimed property. 14

So breakage is real. But it is not the main prize.

The Visit

The main prize is you, walking through the door.

A gift card is a customer the brand did not have to find.

Someone else chose the store. Someone else paid.

And a fifty dollar card almost never buys exactly fifty dollars.

Spend forty-six, and four dollars stay behind. 15

Spend sixty, and the brand just sold you ten dollars more. 15

Either way, the brand wins.

That is why a brand will pay to hang in someone else's store.

And it is why gift cards keep topping wish lists.

In October 2026, forty-seven percent of holiday shoppers told the National Retail Federation they wanted gift cards. More than any other gift. 16

The Middleman

So Blackhawk must be a gold mine.

Not exactly.

In 2017, the last year it published full accounts, more than sixteen billion dollars was loaded through its retail channels. 17

Its revenue was two point two billion dollars. 3

And that year, it reported a loss of one hundred fifty-six million. 18

Because the middleman is squeezed from both sides.

One grocery chain, Kroger, made up ten percent of its revenue. 19

And one brand, Apple, accounted for eleven percent. 20

Lose either one, and a tenth of the business walks out.

And the biggest stores can ask for a bigger share. 21

What Blackhawk really owns is the wiring.

A card on the rack is dead plastic. It comes alive when the cashier scans it, and a signal runs from the till, through Blackhawk, to the brand. 3

Tens of thousands of stores, wired to hundreds of brands. That is hard to copy. 3

In 2018, private investors agreed to buy the whole company, for about three point five billion dollars, including debt. 22

It stopped publishing its accounts. That is why the newest public numbers are from 2017.

One of its biggest rivals, InComm, is privately owned too. 23

And the rack is moving onto your phone. In 2017, Blackhawk bought CashStar, which helps retailers sell their own gift cards, digital and plastic. 3

The Dark Side

There is a darker side to all this.

A gift card works like cash. Whoever has the numbers on the back has the money.

That makes it a favorite tool of scammers. 24

They pose as the tax office, a bank, or tech support, and tell the victim to buy gift cards and read out the numbers. 24

In 2024, people reported losing two hundred twelve million dollars this way to the Federal Trade Commission. 25

And it is rising. In the first nine months of 2025, reported losses were already close to two hundred million. 26

And most victims never report it. 24

No real agency, and no real company, will ever ask to be paid in gift cards. 24

For You

So what does all this mean for you?

One. Treat a gift card like cash, because that is what it is.

Two. Spend it soon. Every month in the drawer is a free loan to the brand.

Three. Check your drawers. The law gives you at least five years, and often more. 12

Four. If a caller tells you to pay with gift cards, hang up. It is a scam. 24

And the next time you stand at a checkout, look at that rack again.

The Idea

It looks like a wall of presents.

It is really a wall of tiny loans.

Sold by a store that does not own the brands.

Wired together by a company most people have never heard of.

And paid for, up front, by you.
