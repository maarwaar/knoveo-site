TheJunkyardEmpire

How Copart makes money: the salvage auction business behind totaled cars, explained.

The short answerWhen a car is written off, the insurer pays the owner and hands the wreck to an auction company. The biggest is Copart. It sells more than 4 million vehicles a year in online auctions, to buyers in over 185 countries. It does not own most of those cars. It charges a fee to the seller and a fee to the buyer, and in its 2026 fiscal year it kept about 32 cents of every dollar as profit: $1.48 billion on revenue of $4.67 billion.

Questions

What happens to a car after it is totaled?

The insurer pays the owner the value of the car and takes ownership of the wreck. It is usually towed to a salvage yard and sold at auction, to dismantlers who sell the parts, rebuilders who repair it, or exporters who ship it abroad.

How does Copart make money?

Mostly from fees. Copart does not own most of the vehicles it sells. It charges the seller and the buyer a fee on each sale, plus charges for towing and storage. About 85 cents of every dollar of its revenue is a service fee.

Why are more cars being written off?

Cars are older, so they are worth less, and they carry more sensors and cameras, so they cost more to repair. When the repair bill gets close to the value of the car, the insurer writes it off.

The whole story

The narration of the film, word for word. Numbers link to their sources.

Cold open

This car has about three seconds left.

Wrecked.

Totaled.

Worthless.

Except, it is not worthless.

The insurance company pays the owner, and takes the car.

And then, the car disappears.

Not into a crusher.

Into a giant yard, owned by a company you have probably never heard of.

There, it is sold in an online auction.

To buyers in more than one hundred and eighty-five countries. 1

That company sells more than four million vehicles a year. 2

It keeps about thirty-two cents of every dollar as profit. 3

That is one and a half billion dollars a year, from cars nobody wanted. 4

And it all started in 1982, with one junkyard in California. 5

So how did one junkyard become all of this?

This is Copart.

And this is the junkyard empire.

The Math

Start with a strange fact.

In America, almost one in four car insurance claims now ends with the car being written off. 6

That is the highest share ever recorded. 7

Insurers call this a total loss.

It does not mean the car is destroyed. It means the repair costs too much, compared with what the car is worth.

Three years earlier, it was closer to one in five. 7

The reason is simple math.

Say your car is worth eight thousand dollars.

And the repair bill comes to six thousand.

That is not unusual. In 2025, the average repair bill was close to five thousand dollars. 7

For the insurer, fixing it makes no sense.

It is cheaper to pay you, and sell what is left.

And two things keep pushing more cars over that line.

First, cars are getting older. The average American car is now almost thirteen years old. 8

Older cars are worth less, so it takes less damage to total them.

About seven in ten totaled cars are at least seven years old. 7

Second, cars are packed with sensors and cameras, and even a small crash can mean an expensive repair. 7

More than a quarter of repair estimates now include recalibrating those sensors. 9

So every year, millions of damaged cars need somewhere to go.

The Junkyard

In 1982, a thirty-four year old named Willis Johnson bought a junkyard in Vallejo, California. 5

He had already spent ten years in the salvage business. 10

He grew up in Oklahoma, with a father who tried everything, from dairy farming, to restaurants, to building houses. 11

He started with a partner, and bought him out in 1986. 10

At the time, junkyards were small, local, and messy.

Johnson saw something different.

Insurance companies did not want wrecked cars. They wanted them gone, quickly, and for the best price.

So he built a business around that one job.

By 1993, one yard had become eleven. 12

Then, in 1994, he did something junkyards did not do. He took the company public. 5

It sold two point three million shares, at twelve dollars each. 13

The money paid for more yards, all across the country.

A year later, Copart bought a rival auction group, and doubled its number of yards in a single deal. 14

By 1997, there were fifty. 10

Fortune magazine ranked it among the one hundred fastest growing companies in America. 10

But the biggest move was still to come.

The Internet Bet

Back then, a salvage auction was a crowd of local buyers, standing in a dusty lot.

Only the people who could drive to the yard could bid.

If it rained, fewer buyers showed up. And fewer buyers meant lower prices.

In 1998, Copart let buyers bid over the internet. 15

It says it was the first in its industry to put photos of the cars online. 15

A buyer no longer had to see the car. They only had to see the screen.

And in 2003, it went all the way.

It became the first to move every sale online, and it ended its live auctions. 5

That first online system went on to sell more than ten million vehicles. 16

The same year, Copart opened its first yard outside the United States, in Canada. A few years later, it reached Britain. 5

Now a wrecked car in Texas could be sold to a buyer on the other side of the world.

More bidders meant higher prices.

Higher prices meant insurers got more money back.

And that meant insurers sent Copart even more cars.

Today, about one million members, in more than one hundred and eighty-five countries, can bid. 17

On any given day, more than one hundred and twenty-five thousand vehicles are up for auction. 18

The Money

Here is the part that makes this business so unusual.

Copart does not own most of the cars it sells.

The cars belong to the sellers, mostly insurers. Copart just runs the sale. 19

About eight in ten of those vehicles come from insurance companies. 20

The rest come from rental fleets, lenders and dealers.

Because it does not buy most of the cars, it does not need much money tied up in stock.

And it charges a fee on both sides.

The seller pays.

The buyer pays.

It also charges for towing, and for storage.

And when cars sell for more, Copart usually earns more too. 21

Add it up, and about eighty-five cents of every dollar of revenue is a service fee. 22

It is less like a car dealer, and more like a toll booth.

Last year, that toll booth collected four point six seven billion dollars. 4

And it kept one point four eight billion as profit. 4

In just nine months, it spent more than one point six billion dollars buying back its own shares. 23

The Land

So why has nobody copied it?

The answer is under the cars.

Wrecked cars need space. A lot of space.

Storage is the real product. An insurer needs a wreck collected today, not next week.

By its own count in 2022, Copart's yards covered roughly sixteen thousand acres. 24

In a single year, it added about twelve hundred more. 25

And the company has always preferred to own that land, not rent it. 25

That matters, because very few towns welcome a new junkyard next door.

Today it runs more than two hundred and fifty locations, in eleven countries. 4

About eighty-three cents of every dollar still comes from the United States. 20

A rival would need the land, the buyers, and the insurers, all at once.

The Storms

Then there are the storms.

When a hurricane floods a city, it can destroy hundreds of thousands of cars in a few days. 26

Hurricane Katrina damaged around six hundred thousand vehicles. 27

After Hurricane Sandy, in 2012, Copart picked up more than eighty-five thousand vehicles in ninety days. 16

Three days after Sandy, insurers had already assigned it more than six thousand cars. Three days after Katrina, the number was three hundred and fifty-nine. 28

At the peak, it collected more than two thousand three hundred cars in a single day. 16

Every day, the wrecks filled almost eleven more acres. 16

It brought in workers from as far away as England, and opened fourteen temporary yards. 16

That response cost the company more than thirty-six million dollars. 16

But it is a big reason insurers keep coming back.

Today, Copart says its storm teams move in within one to two days of a storm passing. 29

After Hurricane Ian, it says it recovered hundreds of thousands of flooded vehicles. 18

The Buyers

So who actually buys a wrecked car?

The members range from scrapyards and repair shops, to ordinary people hunting for a cheap project. 30

Some buyers take them apart, and sell the parts.

Some rebuild them, and put them back on the road.

Even a flooded car is not always finished. Carfax has estimated that about half of flood damaged cars are driven again. 31

And some are shipped overseas, where labor is cheaper and repairs cost less.

The same car that was worthless in one country, is worth fixing in another.

The Catch

It is not a perfect business.

It has one big rival, Insurance Auto Auctions (IAA), which was bought in 2023 for about seven point three billion dollars. 32

And it depends on something it cannot control. How many cars crash, and how many are insured.

Last year, Copart's profit per share slipped, from a dollar fifty-nine to a dollar fifty-five. 4

The company said insurance volumes fell, as claims softened and drivers changed how they insure their cars. 23

In its latest quarter, revenue still grew. But profit fell by about seventeen percent. 33

And the founder is no longer in charge. Willis Johnson stepped down as chief executive in 2010, and handed the job to his son in law. 34

He later told the whole story in a book, called Junk to Gold. 5

The Idea

Still, the idea has barely changed in forty years.

Take the thing nobody wants.

Find the person who does.

And charge a fee to both.

That is how one junkyard became an empire.
